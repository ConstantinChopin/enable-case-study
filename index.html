Case study · Product design

Enable

A single source of truth for luxury travel.
Constantin Chopin · Founding product designer · 2025–2026
The problem

Every tool owns one vertical. Nobody was trying to bring it together.

  1. Some of them make you productive: itinerary builders, booking systems, the tools that get a trip out of the door.
  2. Some of them hold one vertical of your data and only that one: your clients, your itineraries, your drive, your terms.
  3. Nobody was building the place where all of it meets. So every advisor assembled that picture themselves, by hand, on every single trip.

And this is what that looks like

The tools an advisor already had
Four of the six, and no two built in the same decade. None of them holds the commission, the amenities and the client at the same time — so the advisor is the integration layer.

An advisor’s sale runs across eleven stages. We built into the first five.

Where we built
By stage five the advisor has to produce that document on the right. It is the artefact that makes a recommendation official — the thing a client reads and books from. Everything in it is assembled by hand, across the six systems on the left. That is the stretch we built into: it is where the knowledge work concentrates, and where nobody else was building. Every hour spent before stage five is an hour nobody pays for.

The people we designed with

The design partner · weekly, for eight months
The founder who ran the agency on the intranet we were replacing, the operations lead who owned its records, and the advisors whose weeks disappeared into unpaid proposal work. Every decision in the second half of this deck traces to something one of them said in a room.

Six systems in. One model. Every surface reads from it.

What the agency bought
Not answers — a place that absorbs rate sheets as PDFs, inventories as half-structured spreadsheets and terms buried in forwarded email, and returns one record per thing. On the right is a single record out of 4, the directory. The next five slides are the other surfaces, one each.

The constraint

Two user calls a week. Shipping weekly, sometimes twice.

  1. Design ahead of code could not hold that pace. The first build is what happens when it tries.
  2. So the iteration surface became working software. Four repositories, with the prototype using the design system as a live dependency.
  3. The roadmap held the decisions. The prototype was the specification. There was always something an advisor could use on Thursday.

The first build

June 2025. It demoed well — the conversation flowed and the shelf filled up.
What came back

The cards had nothing underneath them.

Hotel name. Location. Description. Asked for a Japan itinerary it returned cherry-blossom festivals, cited to Reddit and Wikipedia, when what was needed was the agency's own negotiated terms.
What we changed

Retrieval was never the problem.

And it was never one body of data. Three different kinds were sitting in the same folders pretending to be one, and nothing said which was which.

So the model came before any screen: three layers, a source on every field, and permission built in rather than added on.

  • Canonical industry data — what a property is, published once and shared by every agency. Nobody at the agency owns it, and nobody there may edit it. The Enable directory
  • Agency data — the negotiated terms, the programme, the rep firm. This is the commercially sensitive layer, and it is the reason none of it could be switched on until they knew who would see what. 93% of the amenities text here was template boilerplate
  • User data — an advisor’s own notes and what they have promised a client. Private unless they say otherwise, and never the agency’s position. Commission was arriving under travellers’ names
The model, and why it came before the screens

Three layers, and permission in the model rather than on top of it.

  • The failure Documents in, answers out, wrong too often to use. Pointing a model at the agency’s files gave confident answers that were wrong often enough that an advisor would stop trusting the whole product inside a week. And when one was right, it was sometimes right about a commission the reader was not entitled to see.
  • The move We made permission a property of the record, not a filter on the output. Everything lands private by default. Every reverse lookup is gated by the record’s own sharing rules, and commission sits behind its own entitlement on top of that. An answer cannot leak what the model will not join in the first place.
  • The measure We wrote the target so it could fail. Time to Trusted Answer: an answer only counted if it arrived with its sources, its freshness, a confidence label or a plain “I don’t know” — and zero permission leakage. Any override is auditable.
  • On the left Three layers, one read-out, and no silent overwrite. Canonical underneath, the agency’s position over it, the advisor’s own on top. What you read is the three combined with every row still attributed — and a change never rewrites the layer beneath it. That refusal is the whole guarantee.

The rest of the time

Three design challenges we worked through.

  1. A person in the loop on the way inUnstructured material from a dozen sources, in a dozen formats, and somebody has to agree how it maps onto a record.
  2. A workspace rather than a dashboardWe built the layout every tool in this category uses, and it fought the way advisors actually move.
  3. Changing a record at the right levelAn advisor edits a field. Who that change is for decides where it is stored and whether it needs approval.
Each one runs the same beats: the first attempt, what came back from the desk, and where we landed.
Challenge 1 · how you get here

A document lands, or a system syncs. Either way a person has to confirm what came out.

An advisor forwards a rate sheet into the vault, or an administrator connects a portal. The extractor proposes records off the back of it, and the vault says so — 3 records proposed from the vault, waiting to be confirmed — which is the way through to the queue on the right. Each candidate keeps the file it came from, gdrive://dmc-kyoto-2026.xlsx, portal://meridian, and one row is held rather than proposed because the source line was unreadable. Nothing reaches an answer, a card or a search until a named person confirms it. That rule was not negotiable, so the only question was what confirming one should cost.
Challenge 1 · first attempt

We let them check every field by hand. This is what that cost.

Field 11 of 87. Record 3 of 214. A completely reasonable request from people whose business depends on the terms being right.
Challenge 1 · what came back

It worked, and nobody was ever going to finish it.

A review that exhausting does not happen. And a review that does not happen is worse than no review at all, because everybody assumes it did.

Challenge 1 · where we landed

The product fills it in, says how sure it is, and a person signs it off.

  1. Every field arrives already filled, with the line it was taken from and where in the document that line sits.
  2. Each one carries how confident the extraction is, so an advisor knows which rows to actually look at.
  3. Two rows here are held rather than proposed — a converted figure with no source currency, and boilerplate copy. A held row has no confirm button at all.
  4. Confirming stamps the record with the name of the person who confirmed it and the date. Every value on this record can be traced to somebody who agreed to it.
Challenge 2 · first attempt

A left rail, and the conflict on its own page.

The pattern every comparable tool in this industry uses. It worked.
Challenge 2 · where we landed

We stopped building a dashboard and started building a workspace.

  1. The rail cost about 230 pixels of width permanently, on the screens that most need it — a record with three layers and a provenance column.
  2. Advisors did not move section to section like a website. They moved workspace to workspace, all day, in a loop.
  3. Each room became its own small thing rather than a section of a dashboard — closer to how these advisors already use a phone than how they use a website.

The cost: about 70 pixels of height, permanently, and a pattern rare enough in this category that it only reads as considered if it behaves well.

Challenge 3 · the problem

The same edit can mean three different things.

An advisor corrects a rate. That might be a note to themselves, or something their desk needs, or the agency changing its official position — and whoever reads it next has to know which. The first version gave them a text box and stored whatever they typed. It never asked who the change was for.

Challenge 3 · where we landed

Editing a field is a question about who it is for.

The canonical value stays underneath and stays readable — a change is stored above it, never over it. Scope decides the layer: just me is personal, my team is the desk, the whole agency is the agency’s position and goes to a lead for review, because an advisor can propose that position but not set it. A reason is required, and the line above the button says what will happen before it happens.
Challenge 3 · and the same question about reading

Who a change is for, and who a record is for, are the same question about different verbs.

  1. Private by default. Sharing a traveller is an explicit act, aimed at a named colleague, and it can be withdrawn.
  2. Collaborator Full can edit and run the profiling, but can never re-share or delete. Collaborator Basic is a name and a contact, for an introduction and nothing more.
  3. Spend sits behind the commission entitlement at every tier. Who you trust with a client is a different question from who you trust with the numbers, so they are separate grants.
  4. It began as all or nothing in March, written down with the condition that would reopen it. In April the agency lead met that condition almost word for word, and it became these three.
What I got wrong

Only the first of those three Keep buttons used to exist.

You used this sheet earlier. I wrote the principle behind it myself: when sources disagree, show every one of them and make a person choose. I argued for it against a faster implementation, and I put it in the product’s own words — “Three sources disagree. The product will not pick one.”

Then I shipped it showing all three sources and offering a way to keep only the first. The screen said the advisor decides, and then decided for them. What you are looking at is the version after that was caught.

What I took from it

I checked what the screen offered. I never checked what it committed.

The fix was not hard. All three are selectable, the reason is required, and it is stored with the value and the name of whoever chose it. What took longer was noticing that I had reviewed the affordance and never the write — that a screen can offer the right three options and still record none of them.

A principle in a document does not enforce itself. The screen that states one most loudly is the one to check first, and the thing to check is what it commits.

Reflection

What I would challenge if I picked it up again.

  1. The resolve flow puts real work on the advisor and does not scale. At ten times the records it needs batching and thresholds — without quietly reintroducing the ranking we removed.
  2. The traveller side is the least proven thing here. The surfaces are built; the extraction that feeds them was an outline when I rolled off.
  3. I would read the permission model as an adversary rather than a user. Both leaks I found were of that kind, and I found them late.
