Case study · Product design

Enable

One place for everything a luxury travel agency knows.
Constantin Chopin · Founding product designer · 2025–2026

The problem

How travel advisors work today, why so much of it is done by hand, and where we chose to focus.

The problem

Travel advisors use many tools, and none of them talk to each other.

  1. Some tools help them get work done, like itinerary builders and booking systems.
  2. Other tools each hold one kind of information: clients, trips, files or hotel deals.
  3. No tool brought it all together. So advisors did it by hand, for every trip.

This is what an advisor works with every day.

Their tools today
The top three are all about the same hotel. Each one holds a different piece of the story, and nothing connects them. The advisor has to connect them in their head.

A sale goes through eleven steps. We focused on the first five.

What we built

The product · Ask

Ask only answers from what the agency knows.

The problem
Advisors did not trust answers from the internet, and could not find what the agency already knew.
What I did
Ask only answers from the agency’s own records. Every answer shows its source and its date, and says “I don’t know” when it has nothing.
The product · The directory

A hotel’s commission and perks are grouped by partner programme.

The problem
For every hotel, advisors had to remember what it paid and what it included, and both change with each partner programme.
What I did
Each programme has its own section on the hotel’s record. You compare them at a glance, then open one to see its full terms.
The product · The briefing

The briefing tells one advisor what their day looks like.

The problem
Advisors started each day not knowing what needed them first. The first version showed a grid of equal cards, with no order to read them in.
What I did
The briefing opens with a few sentences about their day, in order of what matters, then suggests the first thing to do.
The product · The itinerary

Every hotel on a trip shows what it pays and what the client gets.

The problem
Advisors weighed the commission, the perks and the client’s taste in their head, for every hotel on every trip.
What I did
Each hotel on a trip brings its commission and perks with it. Enable warns when a hotel does not suit the client, and blocks hotels the agency has closed.
The product · The traveller

Every client preference shows where it came from.

The problem
Client preferences lived in advisors’ heads and inboxes. And for an independent advisor, the client list is their business.
What I did
Every preference shows where it came from and when. Suggestions wait for a person to confirm them, and the profile stays private to its advisor.
The product · The knowledge vault

Every document comes in through one place.

The problem
Rate sheets, emails and files were spread across many tools, and nobody knew which were current or who could see them.
What I did
Every document comes in through one place, with its source, its date and who can read it. Answers are built only from these documents.

How we got there

How we worked with advisors, the first version we built, what went wrong, and what I learned from it.

How we worked

Two calls a week with advisors. A new version every week.

  1. Designing in Figma first was too slow for that pace. So the working prototype became the design, and advisors always had something new to try.
  2. I made every design decision and wrote each one down: the problem, the evidence, and the option I turned down.
  3. The code followed those decisions. Automatic checks made sure the rules held on every screen.
What we heard

Advisors kept telling us the same thing.

What went wrong

The cards looked right, but there was nothing behind them.

Each card only had a name, a place and a description. Asked for a trip to Japan, it suggested cherry blossom festivals from Reddit. Advisors needed the agency’s own hotel deals.
The key insight

Search was not the problem. Three kinds of data were mixed together.

  • What failed The answers were wrong too often to trust. And some answers showed a commission the reader was not allowed to see.
  • What we found Three kinds of data sat in the same folders. Public hotel facts that nobody at the agency can edit. The agency’s private deals. Each advisor’s own notes.
  • What I changed Every record knows who is allowed to see it. Everything is private until someone shares it. So an answer can never show something the reader should not see.
  • How we measured it Time to a trusted answer. Every answer needs its sources, a date and a confidence level, or a clear “I don’t know”. And it must never show private data.

Four design challenges

The hardest problems we met along the way, and how I solved each one.

The four challenges

Four problems I had to solve.

  1. Checking what comes inDocuments arrive in every format. A person has to confirm what Enable reads from them.
  2. Moving between screensThe usual layout for tools like this did not fit the way advisors work.
  3. Changing a recordWhen an advisor edits something, who is the change for?
  4. Keeping details consistentA reviewer found three inconsistencies on one screen that we had missed.
Each one follows the same steps: what I tried first, what I learned, and where I landed.
Challenge 1 · checking what comes in

A document arrives, and a person confirms what Enable read from it.

Three new records are waiting. One file could not be read, so Enable does not guess. Nothing is used in an answer until a person confirms it.
Challenge 1 · first attempt

First, we let them check every field by hand.

Field 11 of 87. Record 3 of 214. They asked for this, and it made sense: their business depends on the details being right.
Challenge 1 · what I learned

It worked, but nobody was ever going to finish it.

A review that long does not get done. And a skipped review is worse than none, because everyone thinks it happened.

Challenge 1 · where I landed

Enable fills in each field and shows how sure it is. A person approves it.

  1. Every field comes filled in, with the exact cell it came from, like row 41 · col N. The reviewer checks instead of typing.
  2. Each field shows how sure Enable is, so the reviewer knows where to look.
  3. When Enable cannot read something, it does not guess. A person has to fill it in.
  4. Approving adds the reviewer’s name and the date. Every value can be traced back to a person.
Challenge 2 · first attempt

A menu on the left, like every tool in this industry.

It worked. But it did not fit the way advisors move through their day.
Challenge 2 · where I landed

I moved the navigation to a bar at the bottom of the screen.

  1. The left menu took about 230 pixels of width on every screen, including the busiest ones.
  2. Advisors jump between a few places all day, the way they use apps on their phone.
  3. Each place has one name, used everywhere. Every list works the same way, and Back always returns you to where you were.

The trade-off: the bar takes 70 pixels of height. If advisors get lost, my next step is to add a label under every icon.

Challenge 3 · the problem

The same edit can mean three different things.

An advisor corrects a rate. It could be a private note, an update for their team, or a change for the whole agency. The first version never asked which.

Challenge 3 · where I landed

Before you change a field, Enable asks who the change is for.

The original value always stays visible under the change. A change for the whole agency waits for the agency owner to approve it. Advisors can suggest, but they cannot overrule the agency.
Challenge 3 · the same question for sharing

Who can change a record and who can see it follow the same rule.

  1. Everything is private until you share it with a named person. You can undo it at any time.
  2. You can share a full client profile, or only their name and contact details for an introduction.
  3. Spending stays hidden from anyone who cannot see commissions. Trusting someone with a client is not the same as trusting them with the numbers.
  4. In March, sharing was all or nothing. I wrote down what would make me change it. In April, the agency lead asked for almost exactly that.
Challenge 4 · what I learned

A reviewer found three inconsistencies on one screen.

Colours that did not match their legend. Two counts written in different ways. A selected row that did not look selected. We had seen that screen many times. Nothing was checking it.

Challenge 4 · where I landed

Every detail that must stay consistent has a rule, and every rule has a check.

  1. Colour only shows urgency. Amber means a decision is waiting. Red means something is blocked. Everything else is written in words.
  2. Each thing has one name, used the same way on every screen.
  3. A selected row lifts off the page, so you can see it is selected before you act.
  4. 312 automatic checks run across every screen, for both types of user, before each screenshot in this deck.

What comes next

What I would build next, and a last look at the results.

What I would do next

We never built the proposal itself.

  1. A proposal template, so advisors stop building this document by hand.
  2. A copy for the client, so the trip lives in one place instead of a PDF.
  3. Notes during the trip, sent by the advisor inside it, not by email.
