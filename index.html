Case study · Product design

Enable

A single source of truth for luxury travel.
Constantin Chopin · Founding product designer · 2025–2026
The problem

Every tool owns one vertical. Nobody was trying to bring it together.

  1. Some of them make you productive: itinerary builders, booking systems, the tools that get a trip out of the door.
  2. Some of them hold one vertical of your data and only that one: your clients, your itineraries, your drive, your terms.
  3. Nobody was building the place where all of it meets. So every advisor assembled that picture themselves, by hand, on every single trip.

And this is what that looks like

The tools an advisor already had
The top three are one property — Four Seasons Red Sea at Shura Island. The intranet has the opening, the note beside it has the judgement, the partner email has the offer. Nothing joins them, so the advisor is the join.

An advisor’s sale runs across eleven stages. We built into the first five.

Six systems in. One model. Every surface reads from it.

What the agency bought
Not answers. A place that takes PDFs, half-structured spreadsheets and terms buried in email, and returns one record per thing. On the right, one record out of 4.
Surface 3 · Ask

Ask answers from what the agency holds, and says when it cannot.

The advisors told us why a general assistant would go unused: “it’s just information from the Internet.” So an answer is built only from the model, every claim names the record it came from and how old it is, and a question it cannot answer gets “I don’t know”, never a guess from the open web.
Surface 4 · The directory

Commission and amenities are organised by programme, because that is how they are paid.

What breaks by hand is weighing commission, amenities and the traveller at once. A rate only exists under a partner programme, so each programme is a tray: closed, the trays compare; open, one shows its terms — the rate, when it pays, what the guest gets, where the terms came from.
Surface 5 · The briefing

The briefing is written to one advisor, in the order she owes things.

It opens on her day in sentences, with the figures inline — who leaves next, which commissions are late, what she must not book — then walks her obligations in that order and offers one first move. Not a grid of equal cards: a grid cannot be ordered by anyone’s day.
Surface 6 · The itinerary

A line on a trip carries its terms, so choosing is weighing.

Each line books a record and brings its programme: what the guest gets, the rate code, the commission. It is checked against what this traveller likes, and a property the agency has closed cannot be added at all. The three things held in one head, per option, are on the line.
Surface 7 · The traveller

A preference is only as good as where it came from.

Each preference carries its source and date; what the product infers is labelled and never applied until a person confirms it. And the profile is private to the advisor who holds the client, because for an independent advisor the client list is the business.
Surface 8 · The knowledge vault

Everything the model knows came in through one door, with its source and audience.

A rate sheet forwarded in, a portal synced, an intranet exported. Each document keeps where it came from, when, and who may read it, in a word beside an icon, because an answer is only as trustworthy, and as private, as what it was built from.
The constraint, and who decided

Two user calls a week. Shipping weekly, sometimes twice.

  1. A design reviewed in Figma was answering last week’s call by the time it was agreed. So the prototype became the specification, and there was always something an advisor could use on Thursday.
  2. Every design decision was mine to make and to write down: the problem it serves, the evidence, the alternative I turned down, and what would change my mind. The code carried out the log; it never wrote it.
  3. Each rule that has to hold everywhere has a check. A rule nobody checks drifts, and somebody else finds the drift before you do.

What came back

The cards had nothing underneath them.

Hotel name. Location. Description. Asked for a Japan itinerary it returned cherry-blossom festivals, cited to Reddit and Wikipedia, when what was needed was the agency's own negotiated terms.
Why the model came before the screens

Retrieval was never the problem. It was three kinds of data pretending to be one.

  • The failure Documents in, answers out, wrong too often to use. Confident answers, wrong often enough to lose an advisor in a week. And when one was right, it was sometimes right about a commission the reader was not allowed to see.
  • What we found Three kinds of data, in the same folders, unlabelled. Canonical, published once and not theirs to edit. Agency — the negotiated terms. Personal — an advisor’s own notes. Nothing said which was which.
  • The move Permission became a property of the record, not a filter on the output. Private by default, opened up deliberately, never the other way round. An answer cannot leak what the model will not join. Their terms are the one thing an advisor has that the desk next door does not.
  • The measure We wrote the target so it could fail. Time to Trusted Answer: sources, freshness, a confidence label or a plain “I don’t know”, and zero permission leakage. Any override is auditable.

Verify stage and framing
The rest of the time

Four design challenges we worked through.

  1. A person in the loop on the way inUnstructured material from a dozen sources, in a dozen formats, and somebody has to agree how it maps onto a record.
  2. A workspace rather than a dashboardWe built the layout every tool in this category uses, and it fought the way advisors actually move.
  3. Changing a record at the right levelAn advisor edits a field. Who that change is for decides where it is stored and whether it needs approval.
  4. Details that hold on every screenA reviewer found three inconsistencies on one screen. Each had been in front of us, and none had been caught.
Each one runs the same beats: the first attempt, what came back, and where we landed.
Challenge 1 · how you get here

A document lands, or a system syncs. Either way a person has to confirm what came out.

A rate sheet forwarded in, or a portal synced. The extractor proposes records, and three are waiting. One could not be read at all — the source row was unreadable. Nothing reaches an answer until a person confirms it.
Challenge 1 · first attempt

We let them check every field by hand. This is what that cost.

Field 11 of 87. Record 3 of 214. A completely reasonable request from people whose business depends on the terms being right.
Challenge 1 · what came back

It worked, and nobody was ever going to finish it.

A review that exhausting does not happen. And a review that does not happen is worse than no review at all, because everybody assumes it did.

Challenge 1 · where we landed

The product fills it in, says how sure it is, and a person signs it off.

  1. Every field arrives already filled, with the cell it was taken from — row 41 · col N — so the reviewer is checking a claim against a source rather than typing.
  2. Each one carries how confident the extraction is, so an advisor knows which rows to actually look at.
  3. Eighteen fields here, three held rather than proposed. A held row has no confirm button at all.
  4. Confirming stamps the record with the name of the person who confirmed it and the date. Every value on this record can be traced to somebody who agreed to it.
Challenge 2 · first attempt

A left rail, and the conflict on its own page.

The pattern every comparable tool in this industry uses. It worked.
Challenge 2 · where we landed

We stopped building a dashboard and started building a workspace.

  1. The rail cost about 230 pixels of width permanently, on the screens that most need it — a record with three layers and a provenance column.
  2. Advisors did not move section to section like a website. They moved room to room, all day, in a loop, the way they already use a phone.
  3. So a room is named once: the same word in the dock, the crumb and the title. And every list opens the same way — select to preview it beside the list, open to go in — so Back returns you to where you were.

The cost: about 70 pixels of height, and a pattern rare in this category. What would change it: if advisors lose their place between rooms, every room in the dock gets its label first.

Challenge 3 · the problem

The same edit can mean three different things.

An advisor corrects a rate. That might be a private note, something their desk needs, or the agency changing its position — and the next reader has to know which. The first version never asked.

Challenge 3 · where we landed

Editing a field is a question about who it is for.

The canonical value stays readable underneath; a change sits above it, never over it. Scope picks the layer. The whole agency waits for the agency owner to release it — an advisor can propose the agency’s position, not set it.
Challenge 3 · and the same question about reading

Who may change a record, and who may see it, are two halves of one rule.

  1. Private by default. Sharing a traveller is an explicit act, aimed at a named colleague, and it can be withdrawn.
  2. The full profile lets a colleague edit every field, the sensitive ones included, but never share it on or delete it. Name and contact only is for an introduction and nothing more.
  3. Spend sits behind the commission entitlement at every tier. Who you trust with a client is a different question from who you trust with the numbers, so they are separate grants.
  4. All or nothing in March, written with the condition that would reopen it. In April the agency lead met that condition, almost word for word.
Challenge 4 · what came back

Three inconsistencies on one screen, found by somebody else.

A legend whose colours disagreed with the states it named. Two counts on the same page written two different ways. A selected row that did not look selected until you had already acted on it. Each was in front of us. None was caught, because nothing was checking.

Challenge 4 · where we landed

A detail that has to hold everywhere gets a rule, and the rule gets a check.

  1. Colour means severity, and nothing else. Ochre is a decision waiting, claret is a blocker; every other state is a word. A legend cannot disagree with a colour that only ever means one thing.
  2. One name for one thing. The dock, the crumb and the title use the same word, and a count is written the same way wherever it appears.
  3. Selected is lifted, not tinted. The chosen row rises off the page, so it reads as chosen before you act on it, not after.
  4. Each rule has a check that runs before anyone looks: 312 assertions across 24 screens and both roles, green before every capture in this deck.
What I would do next

We never built what stage five produces.

  1. A templated presentation. The advisor assembles this document by hand, every trip.
  2. The client’s own copy. One place their trip lives, instead of a PDF in a thread.
  3. Notes arriving in it. From their advisor, while the trip is happening — not by email.
