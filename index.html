Case study · Product design

Enable

A single source of truth for luxury travel.
Constantin Chopin · Founding product designer · 2025–2026
The problem

Six categories of tool, and none of them holds the whole thing.

  1. Itinerary builders hold the trip. Booking systems hold the reservation. CRMs hold the client. Consortium portals hold the terms.
  2. The agency's intranet had been pressed into service as a suite it was never meant to be.
  3. Nothing sat in the middle, so every advisor rebuilt the same picture by hand, every time.

And this is what that looks like

The tools an advisor already had
Four of the six, and no two built in the same decade. None of them holds the commission, the amenities and the client at the same time — so the advisor is the integration layer.

Eleven stages, and every one touches all six

Where we built
We built the opening stretch, everything up to the proposal — where the knowledge work concentrates and where nobody else was building. Every hour saved before stage five is an hour that was never billable.

The people we designed with

The design partner · weekly, for eight months
The founder who ran the agency on the intranet we were replacing, the operations lead who owned its records, and the advisors whose weeks disappeared into unpaid proposal work. Every decision in the second half of this deck traces to something one of them said in a room.

Six systems in. One model. Every surface reads from it.

What the agency bought
Not answers — a place that absorbs rate sheets as PDFs, inventories as half-structured spreadsheets and terms buried in forwarded email, and returns one record per thing. On the right is a single record out of 4, the directory. The other four surfaces are what I am about to open.
The constraint

Two user calls a week. Shipping weekly, sometimes twice.

  1. Design ahead of code could not hold that pace. The first build is what happens when it tries.
  2. So the iteration surface became working software. Four repositories, with the prototype using the design system as a live dependency.
  3. The roadmap held the decisions. The prototype was the specification. There was always something an advisor could use on Thursday.

The first build

Chat over a pile of documents, results as cards.

June 2025. It demoed well — the conversation flowed and the shelf filled up.
What came back

The cards had nothing underneath them.

Hotel name. Location. Description. Asked for a Japan itinerary it returned cherry-blossom festivals, cited to Reddit and Wikipedia, when what was needed was the agency's own negotiated terms.
What we changed

Retrieval was never the problem.

The agency’s own material was not a trustworthy thing to search either. Once we looked at what was in it, the work stopped being retrieval and started being structure, attribution and permission.

So the data model came before any screen, and chat became one door among several rather than the product.

  • 93% of the amenities text across 3,280 properties was template boilerplate — “View Hotel — experience refined luxury…” — not editorial anyone had written. Database review · March 2026
  • Commission was arriving under a traveller’s name instead of the agency’s, against properties that did not resolve to anything: “Currency Cloud”, “Pipery Hotel”. Advisor · 23 April
  • And none of it could be switched on until the agency knew who would see what: “we want to make sure we’re not sharing sensitive data… as far as client data.” The agency lead · 30 April
Why the data model came before the screens

Permission had to live in the data model. A filter on the answer was never going to hold.

  • The failure Documents in, answers out, wrong too often to use. Pointing a model at the agency’s files gave confident answers that were wrong often enough that an advisor would stop trusting the whole product inside a week. And when one was right, it was sometimes right about a commission the reader was not entitled to see.
  • The move We made permission a property of the record, not a filter on the output. Everything lands private by default. Every reverse lookup is gated by the record’s own sharing rules, and commission sits behind its own entitlement on top of that. An answer cannot leak what the model will not join in the first place.
  • The measure We wrote the target so it could fail. Time to Trusted Answer: an answer only counted if it arrived with its sources, its freshness, a confidence label or a plain “I don’t know” — and zero permission leakage. Any override is auditable.
  • On the left The sharing tiers are that model showing up on a screen. In March we made sharing all or nothing, and wrote down what would reopen it. In April the agency lead met that condition almost word for word, so it became Full and Basic. Money stayed behind its own permission at every tier.

The rest of the time

Six design challenges. Every one of them we got wrong first.

  1. Controlling what gets ingestedThe agency wanted to approve every field on every record before it existed.
  2. Facts that stop being trueA rate, a closure, a commission — each with a day it expires, and nobody watching for it.
  3. Saying how far to trust a valueThe product knew how sure it was. It had no honest way to tell an advisor.
  4. When the sources disagreeThree systems, three commission rates, and no authority to settle it.
  5. Where a decision belongsWe built the layout every tool in this category uses, and it fought the work.
  6. Holding the product to its own ruleWe wrote down that the advisor decides, then shipped a screen that decided for them.
Each one runs the same beats: the first attempt, what came back from the desk, and where we landed.
Challenge 1 · how you get here

A document lands, or a system syncs. Either way a person has to confirm what came out.

The other way in is a connection an administrator sets up, and it lands in the same queue. Each candidate keeps the file it came from — gdrive://dmc-kyoto-2026.xlsx, portal://meridian — and one row is held rather than proposed, because the source line was unreadable. Nothing here reaches an answer, a card or a search until a named person confirms it. That rule is not negotiable, so the only question was what confirming one should cost.
Challenge 1 · first attempt

We let them check every field by hand. This is what that cost.

Field 11 of 87. Record 3 of 214. A completely reasonable request from people whose business depends on the terms being right.
Challenge 1 · what came back

It worked, and nobody was ever going to finish it.

A review that exhausting does not happen. And a review that does not happen is worse than no review at all, because everybody assumes it did.

Challenge 1 · where we landed

We kept the check, and made it something a person could actually finish.

  1. A fixed list of questions a travel designer actually needs answered, rather than every field in the schema.
  2. Each proposed answer arrives as one card: the claim, the quoted line, the page.
  3. Two rows here are held rather than offered — a converted figure with no source currency, and boilerplate copy. Held rows have no confirm button.
  4. When a document is silent, that is recorded as silence. An absent answer is not a no.
Challenge 2 · first attempt

Advisories with an end date.

A notice tells an advisor about a temporary change — a renovation, a paused benefit, a new rate. Somebody has to clean these up, so we gave them an expiry.

Challenge 2 · what came back

An advisor told a client a spa was open. It had been closed six weeks.

The source was real. The citation pointed at the right document. A notice about the closure had an end date, the date passed, the notice removed itself — and the product went back to reporting the last thing it knew, with full confidence.
Challenge 2 · where we landed

The end date came off.

An advisor closes a notice by hand, and the product asks them to confirm it is still true when it goes stale.

Product decision log · 31 March 2026 Temporary advisories: no auto-expire. Advisories stay active until manually closed. Build a stale-advisory review nudge.

The cost: more noise, and a review queue. We took the annoying failure over the invisible one every time this came up.

Challenge 3 · first attempt

A confidence percentage on every extracted value.

It seemed like the honest thing — showing the machine's uncertainty rather than hiding it.

Challenge 3 · what came back

It was precise about the wrong thing.

It looked like a measurement of whether the value was correct. It was really a measurement of how sure the extraction was about its own reading — and a single opaque number gives an advisor nothing to check. It asks for trust instead of offering evidence.

Challenge 3 · where we landed

We took the number away and gave them things they could check instead.

  1. A count of how many sources agree — a fact we can point at.
  2. A written label in words they already use: signed terms, superseded rate, uncorroborated.
  3. Where a comparison genuinely helps, a bar carries it without putting a decimal on it.
Challenge 4 · first attempt

A ranking rule, to settle conflicts automatically.

Take the most recently signed document, or the most-corroborated value, and show that. Half a day of work, and right most of the time.

Challenge 4 · what came back

There is no authority. The authority is the advisor, and the sources they trust.

Design-partner session — attribution to confirm

Ranking would have been quietly wrong at exactly the rate our source ordering was wrong, and nobody would ever have known which answers.

Challenge 4 · where we landed

We show all three values, and a person has to choose.

Every value keeps its source, its date, and how many others agree with it. The advisor chooses, and the choice is stored at the agency layer so the same conflict is never re-litigated.

Product decision log · 31 March 2026 Show all, advisor resolves. When sources conflict, display every value and let the advisor pick. No auto-resolution.
Challenge 5 · first attempt

A left rail, and the conflict on its own page.

The pattern every comparable tool in this industry uses. It worked.
Challenge 5 · where we landed

We stopped building a dashboard and started building a workspace.

  1. The rail cost about 230 pixels of width permanently, on the screens that most need it — a record with three layers and a provenance column.
  2. Advisors did not move section to section like a website. They moved workspace to workspace, all day, in a loop.
  3. Resolving a rate stopped being its own page. The decision only makes sense while you can still see the fields it sits among.

The cost: about 70 pixels of height, permanently, and a pattern rare enough in this category that it only reads as considered if it behaves well.

Challenge 6 · first attempt

A screen that said the advisor decides, and then decided for them.

I wrote the principle, argued for it against a faster implementation, and put it in the product's own copy. Then I shipped a version of the resolve sheet where only one of the three values could actually be kept.

Challenge 6 · what came back, twice

I fixed what the screen offered. I did not check what it committed.

All three values are selectable now, and keeping one requires a written reason. But the store still records that the conflict was resolved without recording which value was kept — so the product asks for a justification and discards it. Checking the affordance is not the same as checking the commit, and I learned that after I had already written the lesson down.

Challenge 6 · where we landed

A principle in a document does not enforce itself.

And the place it fails is the screen that claims it most loudly. I now check the screens that state a principle first, rather than last — and I check what they commit, not only what they offer.

Reflection

What I would challenge if I picked it up again.

  1. The resolve flow puts real work on the advisor and does not scale. At ten times the records it needs batching and thresholds — without quietly reintroducing the ranking we removed.
  2. The traveller side is the least proven thing here. The surfaces are built; the extraction that feeds them was an outline when I rolled off.
  3. I would read the permission model as an adversary rather than a user. Both leaks I found were of that kind, and I found them late.
