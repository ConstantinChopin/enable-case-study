Enable · 2025–2026

Six categories of tool, and none of them holds the whole thing.

Luxury travel agencies run on independent advisors assembling trips out of an industry-wide web of systems that were never designed to meet.

  1. Itinerary builders hold the trip. Booking systems hold the reservation. CRMs hold the client. Consortium portals hold the terms.
  2. The agency's intranet had been pressed into service as a suite it was never meant to be.
  3. Nothing sat in the middle, so every advisor rebuilt the same picture by hand, every time.
Role
Founding product designer, sole designer
When
May 2025 – June 2026
Team
Six, plus a design-partner agency of about fifty advisors

The production product is under NDA. What I am driving is a rebuild of the flows I designed; every name and figure on screen is invented.

The work

Eleven stages, and every one of them touches all six.

  1. Advisors are trained on an eleven-stage cycle, from first lead to post-travel review.
  2. We built for the opening stretch — everything up to the proposal. That is where the knowledge work concentrates, and where nobody else was building.
  3. To place one hotel an advisor plays three things against each other: whether the property suits this client, what the client gets on arrival, and what the advisor is paid.
  4. Book through the consortium and the commission rises but the client loses the breakfast. Book direct and it inverts.
The first build · June 2025

We built the obvious thing first.

  1. Pull every document into a retrieval system, put a chat on top, show what comes back as cards.
  2. It demoed well. The conversation flowed and the shelf filled up.
  3. At a glance it looked like a product.
The first build · the tell

Then you looked at the cards.

  1. The fields read Hotel name, Location, Description — there was nothing structured underneath to fill them with.
  2. Asked for a Japan itinerary it returned cherry-blossom festivals, cited to Reddit and Wikipedia, when what was needed was the agency's own negotiated terms.
  3. An advisor who quotes a lapsed rate loses money. One who promises a withdrawn benefit loses a client. Neither is recoverable by saying the model was confident.
The reversal

A pile of documents is not a record.

  1. Retrieval was never the problem. The knowledge already existed and was already accurate — it was sitting unread in inboxes and portals.
  2. What was missing was structure, a source on every fact, and a permission attached to it.
  3. So the data model came before any screen. Chat survived, demoted to one door among several rather than the product itself.
The engine

The thing the agency actually bought.

Not answers. A single place that could absorb heterogeneous, unstructured material and turn it into records.

  1. In: rate sheets as PDFs, inventories as semi-structured spreadsheets, terms buried in forwarded email, notes in a drive.
  2. Out: one record per thing, with the source, the position in that source, and the date on every field.
  3. This is what an agency cannot build for itself, and it is why they connected their drive and their email before they committed to anything else.
What they asked for

They wanted to check every field by hand. We built exactly that.

  1. A completely reasonable request from people whose business depends on the terms being right.
  2. Eighty-seven fields per record, two hundred and fourteen records in the batch.
  3. It worked, and nobody was ever going to finish it. A review that exhausting does not happen — and a review that does not happen is worse than none, because everybody assumes it did.
What we shipped instead

We kept the rule and changed its shape.

  1. The system captures basic metadata silently, then works through a fixed list of questions a travel designer actually needs answered.
  2. Each proposed answer arrives as one card carrying the claim, the quoted line and the page. Confirm or correct with a keystroke.
  3. Two fields here are held rather than offered — a converted figure with no source currency, and boilerplate marketing copy. Held fields have no confirm button at all.
  4. When a document is silent on a question, that is recorded as silence. An absent answer is not a no.
Guarantee one · provenance

Three layers, and none of them overwrites another.

  1. Canonical — the industry's version of the property.
  2. Agency — what this agency negotiated over twenty years, sitting on top without replacing what is beneath it.
  3. Personal — what one advisor knows and owns. Private by default.
  4. Three rather than one because advisors move between agencies, and agencies hold terms their competitors must never see. A single merged record makes both impossible.
Guarantee one · provenance

When the sources disagree, a person decides.

  1. The partner portal says 12%. The booking platform says 10%. Somebody keyed 14% by hand in April.
  2. Ranking them would have been half a day of work and right most of the time.
  3. It would also have been quietly wrong at exactly the rate our source ordering was wrong, and nobody would ever know which answers.

Decision log · 31 March 2026 Show all, advisor resolves. When sources conflict, display every value and let the advisor pick. No auto-resolution.

Guarantee one · provenance

The decision, in the product.

  1. Every value keeps its source, its date, and how many others agree with it.
  2. A plain label rather than a score: signed terms, superseded rate, uncorroborated.
  3. The panel states where the kept value will land before you keep it — the directory row, the quotes, the answers in chat.
  4. Committing requires a written reason.
Guarantee two · permission

Their terms stay theirs.

  1. Same record, same day, two people at the same desk.
  2. The commission row is not greyed out and it does not carry a padlock. It is not there.
  3. Neither is the other advisor's private note, the agent terms, or the active promotion.
Guarantee two · permission

Masking, refused.

  1. A locked row still tells you the number exists, roughly where it sits, and that it is worth asking about.
  2. For an agency whose most guarded asset is its negotiated terms, that was the difference between connecting their drive and not connecting it.
  3. The cost is that absence then has to mean exactly one thing. Everything else that is empty has to say why it is empty.
Guarantee three · refusal

What an answer has to carry.

  1. A citation — the document, the line, the page.
  2. A date — when this was last known to be true.
  3. A count of how many sources agree.
  4. A permission check on the person asking. If it cannot meet all four, it does not answer.
Guarantee three · refusal

Saying “I don't know” is a designed screen, not an error.

  1. Sources passed. Freshness failed — the newest is over a year old. Corroboration failed — neither source confirms the other.
  2. A refusal that says “no results” is worse than a wrong answer, because the advisor still has a client waiting and now has nothing.
  3. So it names which condition failed, by how much, and offers the three things that would fix it.
The time dimension

And once, it was wrong about time.

  1. It said the spa was open, gave the hours, cited a real document, and marked the answer as meeting the contract. The spa had been closed for six weeks.
  2. Somebody had posted a notice about the closure. The notice had an end date. The date passed and the notice removed itself.
  3. With it gone, the product went back to reporting the last thing it had been told, with full confidence.

The working day

The work arrives; the advisor does not go looking.

  1. Money that is late, travellers who leave soon, notices still open, incentives about to expire.
  2. Every widget is a saved view of a real surface, so opening one takes you there with the filter already applied.
  3. Nothing sends itself. When a commission is late the product writes the chase and stops. The advisor reads it, edits it, sends it.
The working day

Chat is a second door onto the same record.

  1. The advisor with an email half-written does not open a record. They ask.
  2. So the same disagreement is reachable in conversation and behaves identically — the moment chat answers differently from the record, both stop being trusted.
  3. The second frame is the same question asked by a colleague. The rate half is declined; the sources panel is not rendered at all.
The working day

One model, reached through different doors.

  1. The directory, the itinerary workspace, a traveller profile, the vault.
  2. Every one reads the same reconciled records, so a rate corrected in one place is the same rate everywhere.
  3. The intelligence appears inside the work rather than behind a search box — an incentive on a hotel card, a preference conflict before the proposal goes out.
Method

The prototype was the spec.

  1. Two user calls a week, shipping weekly and sometimes twice. Design ahead of code could not keep that pace — the first build is what that looks like.
  2. So the iteration surface became working software. Four repositories: production, dev, the design system, and a prototype that used the system as a live dependency.
  3. The roadmap held the decisions; the prototype held the specification. There was always something demoable to put in front of an advisor.
  4. What survived — hardened journeys, states and components — the engineers implemented in production.
Outcome

What changed.

  1. Client request to a personalised first proposal fell to 10–15 hours. The target we set at the start was twenty.
  2. Advisors reported about an hour a day of administrative time returned, mostly manual reconciliation of rule changes arriving by email.
  3. The agency connected its drive and its email, then committed to Enable as the place its structured knowledge lives.

Reflection

What I would challenge if I picked it up again.

  1. The resolve flow puts real work on the advisor and does not scale. At ten times the records it needs batching and thresholds — without quietly reintroducing the ranking we removed.
  2. The traveller side is the least proven thing here. The surfaces are built; the extraction that feeds them was an outline when I rolled off.
  3. I would read the permission model as an adversary rather than as a user. Both leaks I found were of that kind, and I found them late.
